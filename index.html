The short version

It wasn't always cheap. Timing made the difference.

I got my first EV in April and wanted to know what it would really cost to run. I paired the Geely EX5 with an Andersen A3 home charger and Octopus Agile, where electricity prices change every half-hour.

The idea is simple: let charging follow the cheaper slots where possible, rather than automatically filling the battery at any price. Over six months that came to about 1,018 kWh of home charging for a net energy cost of £67.23.

That average hides some big swings. May was the reality check; September was the surprise.

One month the grid effectively paid me to charge the car — the average price went negative.

When I bought my Geely EX5, I expected home charging to be cheaper than running a petrol or diesel car. What I didn't expect was that, occasionally, electricity would effectively pay me to charge it.

I didn't manage this by constantly watching prices or rearranging my life around the cheapest half-hour. The car simply charged whenever the price met the conditions I'd set — and over six months, that was enough to land 42.4% of its energy in negative-price slots.

The basics

So, how does this actually work?

  • Octopus Agile has a different electricity price for each half-hour, with 48 prices across the day.
  • The next day's prices are usually published around 4pm.
  • My Andersen charger uses a price ceiling: it charges when the price is below my limit and waits when it's above it.

And if the price goes negative? That's when things get particularly interesting. During a negative-price period, the electricity unit price is below £0/kWh — in simple terms, the wholesale market is effectively paying for consumption during that period. For an EV, which can shift a large chunk of its electricity use to another time, that's a very useful opportunity.

Those are genuine half-hourly prices from the Octopus app. The midday trough went negative, then the price climbed to a 45p-ish evening peak a few hours later — a swing of nearly 50p/kWh within the same day.

A public rapid charger costs me around 60p/kWh or more, so almost any home price is a win — but on Agile I'm not really chasing "cheap," I'm chasing free or negative.

How I think about the price tiers

Around 7p/kWh or belowGenuinely cheap, but my "can't-wait" fallback if the car needs charge now and I can't wait for a better window.
0p/kWh or belowThe sweet spot: I plan charging into these windows, and sometimes the price is negative, so I'm paid to fill the battery.

So the question isn't "is 5p cheap?" — of course it is. It's "why pay 5p when, a bit of patience later, the same energy is free or negative?" The 7p line is just my limit for when patience isn't an option.

The six-month log

Six months, six different outcomes

The chart is the overview; these are the moments that explain it.

April · The promising start3.9p/kWh avg

79 kWh cost £3.08, with 64% of charging energy in negative-price slots. I was still getting used to the car and the tariff, so I wasn't trying hard — the car just happened to be home when the cheap periods passed through. Not a bad start.

May · The reality check14.6p/kWh avg

242 kWh cost £35.26. Not one kWh landed in a negative slot all month — a proper reality check, and proof this project isn't about making EV charging look artificially cheap. It's about recording what actually happened.

June · Back to bargain territory2.2p/kWh avg

109 kWh cost £2.44, with 49% of charging energy in negative-price slots — the kind of bill that makes you double-check you've entered the numbers correctly.

July · Cheap at higher mileage3.8p/kWh avg

About 800 miles of driving, with 202.4 kWh charged for just £7.72. More driving than June, but I still wasn't chasing every half-hour — the car simply took what was on offer while it was parked.

August · The trip that couldn't wait12.2p/kWh avg

A journey I couldn't move meant the car had to be charged by a fixed day. With not enough already in the battery, and the grid not cheap in the hours before I left, some of that charge had to come at higher prices. The average rose to 12.2p/kWh and the month cost £21.86. If every month looked like June, the story would be too easy — real life doesn't cooperate like that.

September · The surprise−1.5p/kWh avg

207.2 kWh at an average −1.5p/kWh produced a −£3.15 net charging result — not nearly free, actually negative, for the whole month. One session on the 20th alone pulled 57.6 kWh over eight and a half hours, almost entirely at negative prices. I didn't do anything differently; I just left the price ceiling where it was and let the car and the weather sort it out.

Down to the day

When did the negative hours land?

Negative pricing isn't random. Over the six months it has fallen overwhelmingly on weekends and in the middle of the day — when demand is low and there's plenty of wind and solar on the grid. That is also, inconveniently, when the car is most likely to be out.

Saturdays and Sundays alone account for roughly three-quarters of every negative-price hour since April. The two brightest days on the calendar are 19–20 September, when prices were negative almost around the clock.

What I've learned

Flexibility is the real advantage

Let the charger do the waiting

A battery doesn't mind when it fills, only that it's full when I need it. A price ceiling hands that decision to the market instead of a clock, and avoids most expensive slots without me watching prices all day.

Routine matters

If the car is away during a cheap window, that opportunity is missed — and it often is. On weekends 59% of negative half-hours went unused, and on my office days 88% did. Convenience and real driving needs still come first.

One season isn't a forecast

Six months show what's possible, not what every driver will pay — May proved Agile isn't magically cheap. A full year, across all the seasons, will give a fairer picture.

For the detail-minded

Method & caveats

Is the £67.23 an estimate?

No. The total is based on actual charging and prices:

  • £67.23 covers 1,018 kWh of home charging across 6 months. It's built from the actual half-hourly Agile prices tied to the electricity my charger used, not an assumed rate.
  • On Agile, multiplying total kWh by a single average rate can give a very different, misleading answer.
  • Monthly figures are each rounded before display, so they won't always sum exactly to the six-month total. The total itself is calculated from the unrounded underlying data.
Does it include standing charges or public charging?

No. This measures the cost of home charging on Agile:

  • It's the net unit-energy cost of home EV charging only.
  • Household standing charges, public rapid charging, insurance, servicing, tyres and depreciation are excluded. The household electricity comparison above is a separate figure with its own scope.
  • Public rapid-charging sessions on longer trips aren't in this total; including them would lift the real per-mile cost of running the car. Home charging on Agile supplies the overwhelming majority of the car's energy.
How reliable are the figures, and how were the missed slots counted?
  • EV energy is metered directly by the home charger. Every half-hour is priced at the real Octopus Agile rate, then grouped by calendar month.
  • April runs from the 13th only, since that's when this account joined Agile. Figures are aggregated and rounded from the underlying half-hourly data, then cross-checked against Octopus billing statements for April–September 2026.
  • Of missed slots, 37% on office days and 66% on weekends followed a large charge in the prior day or two. That suggests the battery was often already topped up rather than the car simply being away.
  • Of the ten biggest prior top-ups, only four were at positive rates, averaging 3.1p; the rest were at negative or near-zero prices.
